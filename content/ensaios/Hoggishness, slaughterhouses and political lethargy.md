According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, to obtain a sustainable diet, one must eat a wholesome variety of the best available foods and nutrients, ensuring the maximum possible health benefits. “The message is simple: eat real food.” (2026), nevertheless, nearly 90% of health care is spent in treatment for chronic diseases linked to malnourishment, with more than 70% of American adults being overweight or obese. 

In 2025, $57.3 billion to the U.S. economy was subsidized by the meat industry. Even with plant-based foods being advocated by dietary guidelines globally, it is not sufficient to moderate the animal consumption of our current age. 98% of U.S. households purchase meat, or better, a pre-packaged, anonymous, pink pasty mixture that’s so easy to slip into a shopping-cart. In consequence, around 27 billion pounds of pork and beef are processed by the meatpacking industry per year. 

More than a century ago, when the abattoir sector became a mass production, economic entities took advantage of people and public health to maximize profit. Workers suffered, without barely any human conditions, the brutal consequences of backbreaking labour, often debilitating and sometimes even lethal. Despite the literary denunciations of activists, this was neglected and ignored in favor of protests related to poor hygiene. As things evolved, the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) and the Meat Inspection Act (1906) were eventually passed, but they did not resolve, or even attempt to resolve, the laborers' working conditions, revealing a society that views the drama of the meat industry and the consequences of this mass production with worrying partiality.

Contributing to this humanitarian crisis, Charlotte Perkins Gilman is insightful in identifying the ambiguity of the American attitude: one's own death from poor-quality food is urgent, while the high mortality rate inside the factories is irrelevant! Willing to die a “dry death"  instead of showing herself as bigoted as the public, her provocative reflections are the perfect scalpel to dissect this dark and unfinished labor parenthesis. In this blog, in addition to exploring the historical context and characteristics of the workforce in more detail, we will try and answer the question: can the topic of the meatpacking industry be used as leverage to expose political negligence? 

### Chicago, the “Hog Butcher for the World”

The arrival of the meat industry stemmed from a westward shift in population and economic interests following the Civil War. With the construction of an extensive railroad system, Chicago became the epicenter of major livestock yards, established as The Union Stock Yard in 1865. This industrial district, also known as Packingtown, was home to an ethnic and religious mixed neighborhood that hosted entire generations of butchers. A town built around “gullies and ditches, and great hollows full of stinking green water” (Sinclair, 1906) where the children could play, surrounded by “a ghastly odor, of all the dead things of the universe”, a man-made land used as a dumping-ground for garbage. Only this description written in Sinclair’s work _The Jungle_ is sufficient to presuppose the disgusting and poor conditions that the residents lived in. Lithuanians, Sicilians, Slovenes, Ukrainians, Poles and Russian jews, who sacrificed their past lives for the hope of a better salary and a more prosperous future, all in Packingtown's factories, fueling the growth of the town.

Whilst providing jobs to the immigrant population, Packingtown became a culturally enriched place of both mutually supportive communities and disagreements between different national groups. This tangled web was indispensable for the political and social dynamics of Chicago, yet the system dehumanized its workers, since most tasks were little to less demanding and with almost no skill required. A simple cutting motion that could be systematic, denying any glimpse of individuality. As Marx affirms, “in the factory we have a lifeless mechanism independent of the workman, who becomes its mere living appendage” (1977). Such was its influence that, later on, Henry Ford's assembly line was inspired by this method of exploiting the worker: the efficiency of the mechanism appealed to the entrepreneur so much, that the parcelling out of work originated from it.

### Sinclair's "The Jungle" : A Catalyst

Although conditions in slaughterhouses appear to have progressed since beef productions started to require legislations, regulations, besides countless other inspections regarding meatpacking, the procedure that brings an animal to our plate is still fundamentally the same. 

Sinclair’s _The Jungle_ is the result of a long and fruitful investigation rooted in Packingtown. The novel excels in exposing the truth behind this new reality, as the author reports the cruel slaughtering of the hogs, each single one chained to the nearest and simultaneously attached to a ring on a wheel that, when spinned, would make the animals be jerked off their feet and hung in the middle of the air, before “another was swung up, and then another, and another, until there was a double line of them, each dangling by a foot and kicking in frenzy and squealing” (p.40). They would squeal, grunt and wail, “passing on the way through a wonderful machine with numerous scrapers” (p.42) and then again strung, scrapped, cutted swiftly, opened up until every centimeter of the carcass was compressed multiple times and taken to a fridge. “Ten million live creatures turned into food every year” preserved in a cold room, resembling a forest of animal death. 

Furthermore, _The Jungle_ was not solely focused on the cruelty of the slaughter, but also on the workers' struggle as Sinclair tried to denounce the brutal working conditions of the factories. Body parts would either be chopped off or eaten by acid, even worse, entire workers would fall into the vats until there was nothing left to fish out. The desperate plights of these unfortunate people were reflected through a parallel of the animals themselves. “Each one of these hogs was a separate creature”, some were white, black, brown, spotted, old, young, long and lean; but all with “a will of his own, a hope and a heart’s desire” (p.41) as both the animal and the human were likewise cash-in tools for the pockets of unscrupulous businessmen. 

Suffice to say this severely perturbed the Americans, but not for the reasons one might expect. The interest of the public was not on the workers at all, they only had eyes for the quality of the meat that would be served on their plate: the idea of eating human remains on Thanksgiving Day was more intolerable than any of the workers suffering. Sinclair's disappointment was made obvious when he stated “I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident hit it in the stomachs” (Sinclair, 1963).

### Charlotte Perkins Gilman Vs Lethargy

Known for her work as a woman’s rights advocate, activist and poet, Charlotte Perkins Gilman had a prolific career that focused mainly on reproductive freedom, economic independence and gender equality. In addition to these themes, delving deeper into her biography, her literary works of both non-fiction and poetry encompass unforeseen topics of nourishment, individual well-being and conscious eating. For Gilman, even in the food, there was a hidden fight that required reform. 

After Sinclair’s novel, crucial exposure of industrial animal production and amidst the food frenzy of the Americans, Gilman contributes with her own poem _I Would fain die a dry death._  Entitled after Shakespear’s play _The Tempest_, Gilman refers to the honest old councilor's vows:  “Now I would give a thousand furlongs of sea for an acre of barren ground – long heath, brown furze, anything. The wills above be done, but I would fain die a dry death” (p. 149). In other words, the old councilor states he would willingly, or with pleasure, die from dehydration in a 4,047 square metre of brown, sterile land, then to die from drowning. By referencing Shakespear’s character, Gilman is making an allusion to reinforce her own position. Just like the sailor, she would also willingly choose an area of uncultivated land, too poor to produce anything, rather than drowning in complicity with the intentional slaughter or the workers' abuse. What Gilman pictures as a thousand furlongs of sea is the abominable activity of Packingtown. The title serves as the beginning of her denunciation of those who swim unawarely in this sea, due to their own negligence to fully see the gravity of things. 

Gilman doesn’t mince her words: in the poem there is an immediate criticism towards the American audience and, as an American, Gilman's includes herself in the generalization of her public. If there's one element that distinguishes her, it is certainly the use of sarcasm: it's a literary weapon that forces the readers to admit their own shortcomings and responsibilities, by highlighting the absurdity of the circumstances. In a mass that behaves and acts the same, like herds, her voice is deprecating and blunt, exposing the lack of critical thinking and the inability to act for a cause that goes beyond one’s own consumerism.  

The poem begins with Gilman directly accusing the public of not batting an eye at any type of scandal, regardless of the nature or the urgency of the situation, whether it be corruption of the government, hazard of industry or environmental consequences. On the other hand, when food becomes part of the equation, the dormant state is broken and the public finds strength to collaborate as a mass. Despite achieving a union between people, their singular voice is always directed towards its own personal needs, indifferent to social and humanitarian questions. The author addresses her frustration over the people’s priority of canned goods, which makes them inattentive about the monopolisation of livestock markets such as the Beef Trust. 

Gilman goes on to effortlessly mock this consumer, but without minimizing the major hygiene and health problems of meat production. There is a fear of sickness being incorporated in the meat, poisoning the limbs, being purchased unconsciously. She asks the rhetorical question “Can a label command belief?” as if the concern of the consumer would be alleviated when the label is fixed and proper, giving an inviolable certainty, just like our current comforting nutritional scores. 

From the start to the bottom, Gilman’s voice can briefly change from her scornful rebuke to a playful, child-like, nursery rhyme; only to quickly return to her sober critique. It’s in the intertext of these verses that the reader can grasp the complexity of such a problem. If, at a superficial level, there’s a clear criticism about obsession over hygiene, peeling back the layers of the poem, we find Gilman’s denunciation of the public's lethargic attitude. 

Aware of the blinders worn by her audience, she rebukes them to elicit a political response. Gilman’s intention is to free from inertia the meek and amiable Americans, as it is demonstrated in the expression “We do rise up and remonstrate/ Against the Packingtown dirt”: an anthem against the hoggishness, omnipresent in their mentality. Her final two verses are an ironic, funny little taunt, that confines this large-production of disease to a single entity, the so-called Mr. Packer, requesting him, at least, a packed hygienical death. 

### Conclusion: The Gospel of the Butcher


In the end, the activists' work produced during this time period constructed one final picture, exposing the truth behind these social-economical atrocities. To Sinclair’s _The Jungle_ and Gilman's words we can add on Carl Hassman’s cartoon in the _Puck_ magazine, in an amalgamation of art that supports one another in this creative form of protest.  

The drawing is a visual complement to Gilman's poem. It illustrates exquisite products labeled as “Potted Poison, Chemical Corn Beef [...] Tuberculosis Lard, Decayed Roast Beef” and also “Putrefied Pork” sold by a butcher, accompanied by a passage from the gospel, right underneath, which says : 

> Therefore I say unto you, Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink; nor yet for your body, what ye shall put on. Is not the life more than meat, and the body than raiment? (Matthew, 6:25) 

Ripped from the Bible and inserted into this cartoon, Hassman uses this excerpt also sarcastically. Since it justifies an unworried attitude about what we consume, it allows the citizen to not need to think about the produce they buy, free from consciousness. 

All of these critics recycle the same image of rottenness present in the meat industry. There is not an obvious connection between animal agriculture and political indifference, before we slowly begin to uncover how over-indulgence will usurp the body and the mind, until there’s only the thought of selfish consumption. As we mentioned in the beginning, the carnivorous sector is still alive and kicking, stronger than ever: even though today there is greater concern towards physical wellbeing, the reality remains the same: poor conditions, labour exploitation and a mentality purely consumerist. If Gilman, with her poem, teaches us anything, is to join her in a dry death against a hoggish lifestyle. Remembering that, instead of having a gluttony for food, we should be voracious for protesting.
